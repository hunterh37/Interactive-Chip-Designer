Rack to the Future — Technical Spec
Oct 18, 2026  ·  @Jason - MedVR
Summary
Rack to the Future is an Apple Vision Pro app that lets a team design and inspect AI data
centers as one multi-scale digital twin, from a single chip out to a fleet of sites on Earth, in
orbit, on the Moon and on Mars. It is built for the SundAI MIT AI Hackathon challenge to
design the 2035 data center, but works on today's facilities too.
Problem. Data center decisions about chips, cooling, power, buildings, cost and public
reaction live in separate spreadsheets and tools. Nobody sees how a choice at one scale
(a 5.5 kW chip) forces choices at another (liquid cooling, grid capacity, siting).
What the app does. You zoom continuously through the scales, switch analytical lenses at
any scale, and move a time slider from 2025 to 2035. Constraints such as budget, grid cap
and launch cost are adjustable presets, not hard rules, so the trade-offs play out live.
Goals for the prototype
A working zoom from rack to fleet with at least two lenses (heat and power) live at
every level.
A scenario panel where the challenge numbers are default presets the user can
change.
Constraint checks that visibly flag the first limit a scenario hits.
A 3–5 minute demo that tells one clear story.
Non-goals for the hackathon
Engineering-grade CFD or electrical simulation; we use simplified models.
Live integration with real facility telemetry.
A complete optimizer that finds the best fleet; the app shows consequences, people
make the choices.

Users and use cases
The primary user is a planning team (infrastructure, finance, comms) sitting together
around one shared model. The app should answer "what happens if we change this?" in
seconds.
Core use cases:
1. Build a fleet scenario from presets (user case A, B or C; rack mix; sites) and see which
constraint binds first.
2. Zoom from a fleet-level warning down to its cause (for example, a grid overload traced
to one hall's rack mix).
3. Compare 2025, 2030 and 2035 versions of the same facility on the time slider.
4. Walk through a single hall in immersive mode to review layout, heat and cooling with
the team.
Scale ladder
The twin has seven zoom levels, and every level uses the same data model, so a number
changed at one scale flows up and down the ladder. Each level picks the visionOS
presentation that suits its physical size.

Budget: 5-year cost must stay at or under the budget.
Serving: delivered prefill and decode throughput must cover 100% of the chosen case
at peak.
Cooling fit: each rack's power per chip must suit its hall's cooling type (for example, a
792 kW GPU rack cannot be air cooled).
Latency: interactive work must run where round-trip time allows; Moon and Mars only
take background or batch work.
Worked example with the defaults
With 500 MW and no cooling overhead, the grid holds about 631 of the 2035 GPU racks
(792 kW each), which is about $15.0B of hardware. Hardware plus buildings and roughly
$1.1B of energy over 5 years at $0.05/kWh still fits under $30B. So with these presets the
grid binds before the budget does, and further growth has to come from off-world
capacity or from more tokens per watt (for example, LPU racks for decode). This is the
core story the demo shows.
Time horizon
A time slider runs from 2025 to 2035, with 2030 projection data as the midpoint. The same
facility morphs as the slider moves: rack types swap, power density rises, and cooling
options change.
2025: GB200 NVL72 racks (132 kW, 72 GPUs, about $3–5M) as the baseline. This makes
the app immediately useful on real facilities with today's data.
2030: projection data drives the middle of the range; values between known points are
interpolated.
2035: the challenge's GPU, LPU and air racks.
Each rack type and parameter carries a year tag, so a scenario can be replayed at any year.
Moving the slider re-runs the constraint checks, which shows when a design that fits in
2025 stops fitting.
App architecture
The app is built around one pure-Swift scenario engine; every view and lens reads from it,
and only the scenario panel writes to it.

Data model
Scenario holds the parameters and a list of Sites. A Site has a location (Earth, LEO, Moon
or Mars), a grid allocation and Buildings. A Building has Halls; a Hall has a cooling type and
a rack mix. A RackType carries power, chip count, cost and year, and points to a ChipType.
UserCase holds target users, reads per user per day and peak tokens per second.
Models are generated from simple shapes rather than detailed assets, so the scale views
stay fast and the hackathon build stays small.
Demo flow
The demo tells one story: the grid runs out before the money does, and the twin shows
where compute has to go next.
1. Open on the fleet globe with the challenge presets loaded and user case B (agent
developers) selected.
2. Pinch into one Earth site, then a building, then a hall. Walk the hall in immersive mode
with the heat lens on.
3. Pinch into a GPU rack and a chip: 5.5 kW per chip, flagged as needing direct liquid or
immersion cooling. Switch the hall to air cooling to show the red warning, then switch
back.
4. Zoom back out to the fleet and add GPU racks. The grid gauge hits 500 MW while the
budget gauge still has room.
5. Pull the camera back to low Earth orbit. Turn on LEO capacity and watch the launch
cost appear in the business case lens.
6. Drag the time slider back to 2025 to show the same tool on today's GB200 facilities.
7. Close on the title: Rack to the Future.

Open questions and risks
Risks
Device time: only Vision Pro hardware shows the immersive hall properly. Mitigation:
build and test most scales in the simulator, and book device time for the hall.
Scope: seven scales and six lenses is a lot for a hackathon. Mitigation: the build plan's
priority order; rack and fleet scales with heat and power lenses are enough for the
demo.
False precision: simplified models can look more exact than they are. Mitigation: label
assumptions in the scenario panel and round displayed values.
